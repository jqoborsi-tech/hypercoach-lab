Issue 02 — what changed

The same 749 square feet, stretched into a bar

Issue 01 was a near-square block, 28′‑0″ × 27′‑6″. This is the same enclosed area drawn as 38′‑0″ × 21′‑0″ — ten feet longer, six and a half feet shallower, a 1.81:1 bar instead of a 1.02:1 cube. On a 120‑foot-deep lot that is not a stylistic preference; it is what keeps the rear yard usable.

The square block would have reached 33′‑6″ back from the rear property line. The bar reaches 27′‑0″. Against a house whose rear wall sits about 62′ from the street, that is the difference between roughly 24 feet of yard and 31 feet — and the yard is the thing an ADU on a deep lot most easily destroys.

Elongation also sorts the plan by orientation. Every long wall now has one job. The east wall faces the yard and the house: it carries the entry loggia, the 6′ slider, and both bedroom windows. The west wall sits 6′‑0″ off the rear property line: it carries the kitchen run, the bath, and almost no glass. The two short gable ends face the side property lines. Nothing important looks at a fence.

The cost is small and worth stating: net livable area falls from 663 SF to 656 SF, because a longer perimeter means more wall, and the compact 26 SF vestibule of the square plan grows into a 48 SF entry hall to reach both bedrooms across the bar's width. Seven square feet for thirty feet of yard is a trade worth making.

749 is still exact

A 38′‑0″ × 21′‑0″ rectangle is 798 SF. A 7′‑0″ × 7′‑0″ entry loggia notched out of the southeast corner removes 49 SF. 798 − 49 = 749, measured to the outside face of the exterior walls — the strictest reading a plan checker can apply to the 750 SF impact-fee threshold in Gov. Code §66311.5. On the interior-livable measure the city actually uses it is 656 SF, with 94 SF of margin.

The loggia does real work besides arithmetic. It is a 49 SF outdoor room open to the east and south, covered by the main roof, that gives the front door a threshold instead of a hole in a wall.

Setbacks stay at six feet

Santa Ana would allow 4′‑0″. At 6′‑0″ on the rear (west) and north side, with 12″ eaves and rakes on those two faces, every wall and every roof edge holds 5′‑0″ or more from a property line, which puts the whole building outside CRC Table R302.1(1): no one-hour assemblies, no 25% opening cap, no protected soffits. The south side keeps 16′‑0″ for the path of travel and the approach.

What the address lookup found — and did not 3010 S Artesia St is a 1,489 SF, 4-bedroom, 2-bath single-family residence built in 1973, in the 92704 tract off S Artesia. The parcel record itself sits behind the Orange County Assessor and City of Santa Ana property portals, both of which this session’s network proxy blocks, so the lot has not been verified. 3009 S Artesia, directly across the street, is on a 7,218 SF lot; the street averages about 6,300 SF. A 60′ × 120′ parcel is the working assumption here and it is the one number in this set most worth confirming first, because it sets the setbacks, the coverage, and where the bar lands. The site plan is drawn with S Artesia on the east; if 3010 is on the east side of the street, mirror it.

Before submittal

Redlines

Seven things to settle for this parcel. Two of the eight from Issue 01 are now closed: a 1973 tract in 92704 sits outside Santa Ana’s historic districts, and the existing garage and driveway already carry the parking requirement without new paving.

  1. Pull the parcel record. Lot dimensions, the recorded footprint of the house and garage, and any easements. Everything on Sheet A‑1.0 rests on a 60′ × 120′ assumption inferred from the neighbours; the Orange County Assessor and the City of Santa Ana property portal both hold the real numbers and neither was reachable from this session. Get a survey before a permit set regardless.
  2. Confirm SAMC 41‑194 as adopted. The detached maximum floor area, the height ceiling, and whether the city measures ADU area as gross or interior livable space. The design survives the stricter reading of each, but verify line by line against the current ordinance.
  3. Check the transit distance anyway. Not for parking — the driveway covers that — but because a major transit stop within a half mile raises the height ceiling from 16′ to 18′, which would give the bar room for a steeper roof or a taller plate if you want one.
  4. Fire access. The hose pull measures roughly 118′ from the curb along the driveway and side path. Confirm how SAFD measures it on this parcel and that the 3′‑6″ path stays clear of the house eaves and any gate.
  5. Sprinklers. Confirm the 1973 house has no sprinkler system. State law only protects the ADU if the primary dwelling is unsprinklered.
  6. Solar. Have the energy consultant run the array on the CF1R. If it lands at or above the exemption threshold, the west slope carries it — 38 feet of unbroken 4:12 roof, better PV real estate than the Issue 01 scheme had.
  7. Geotechnical and utilities. Parts of Santa Ana sit in a state-mapped liquefaction zone, and a soils report drives the foundation. Separately, price the sewer and water runs: the bar sits about 100′ back from the street, and that trench is a real line item.

What is assumed, and what would change it

A 60′ × 120′ interior lot with S Artesia on the east, a 1,489 SF single-storey house with an attached two-car garage set 20′ back from the front property line, an unsprinklered primary dwelling, no easement across the rear, and utility capacity at the street. The building itself — 38′‑0″ × 21′‑0″ with a 7′ loggia notched out of one corner — drops into any rear yard that can give it 38′ of width and 27′ of depth. A shallower lot pushes it forward and eats the yard; a narrower one forces the bar back toward the square block of Issue 01.
